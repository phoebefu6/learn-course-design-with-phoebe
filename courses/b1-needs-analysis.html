Part 1 · covers the five causes

Five reasons somebody is not doing the thing

7 min live

Only two of them are training. The other three are extremely common, and a course aimed at any of them is a course that will be well received and change nothing - which is worse than doing nothing, because everybody now believes the problem was addressed.

LiveThe question that settles it in ninety seconds4 min▶

"Could they do it if their life depended on it?"

If the answer is yes - if, with enough at stake, they would get it right - then it is not a skill gap, and no amount of training will help. What you are looking at is expectation, tooling, incentive or environment.

Run it on Daybreak: could a senior engineer, told their job depended on correctly classifying this schema change, get it right? Mostly yes. Which tells you immediately that a pure skills course is aimed at maybe a third of the problem.

The follow-up questions, in order

"Has anybody ever told them this is their job?" Often nobody has.
"What happens today if they do it right?" At Daybreak: nothing. The PR takes longer.
"What happens if they do it wrong?" Also nothing, for them - the analytics team absorbs it at 08:40.
That third answer is usually the whole diagnosis, and no course fixes it.

"Can you do an hour on data contracts so this stops happening?" - "Sure."

You build an excellent hour. Everybody attends, the feedback is good, and four months later a producer renames a column and thirty-four assets break. The conclusion drawn is that the training did not work, or that engineers do not care. Neither is true, and you now have less credibility for the next request.

"Before I build it - could they do it if their life depended on it? Has anybody told them it is their job? What happens today if they do it right?"

"Mostly yes. Probably not. Nothing, it just takes longer." So: a 40-minute session for the third who genuinely cannot classify changes, a CI check that fails the build, and one line in the team's definition of done. The session is now a third of the solution, and you said so in advance.

Part 2 · covers saying it

Saying not-only, without it sounding like no

5 min live

The diagnosis is the easy part. The hard part is telling somebody who asked for a course that a course is a third of the answer, in a way that leaves them pleased rather than defensive.

Self-studyThe sentence, and what to do with the other causes4 min▶

The shape that works, borrowed straight from learn-communication-with-phoebe - ask first, then the options:

"Yes, and I think it gets you about a third of the way. The other two thirds are that nobody has told them it is their job, and that today a careful producer just gets a slower pull request. I will build the session. Can you own the definition-of-done line, and shall I ask platform about a CI check?"

  • Say yes first. They asked for something reasonable and refusing it is the fastest way to have the conversation end.
  • Quantify your share. "About a third" is honest, memorable, and sets the expectation you will be measured against in b5.
  • Name the other causes as facts, not criticisms. "Nobody has told them" is a gap; "your team does not care" is an accusation about the same gap.
  • Take one of them yourself. Owning one non-training action is what stops this reading as an excuse, and it is usually the cheapest one.
Build-along

Diagnose a real request

★ 22 min · everyone builds

The gap, behaviourally. Not "they do not understand contracts". What is somebody doing or not doing? "Producers ship schema changes without checking consumers." Now it is observable.

Could they, if it mattered enormously? Yes means it is not a skill gap for most of them. Ninety seconds, and it reframes the whole request.

Five causes. Know-how, expectation, tools, incentives, environment. Mark which apply. Most requests have two or three, and rarely only the first.

Your share. What fraction is genuinely training? Write the number. It will be smaller than the request assumed, and saying it early is what protects you in month four.

The sentence. Yes, and. Name the other causes, take one, ask them to own one. Write it before the meeting.

Write the gap as a behaviour. If your sentence contains "understand", "awareness" or "culture", rewrite it until it names something somebody does or does not do.

Ask the life-depended-on-it question out loud. It feels dramatic and it works because it separates capability from everything else in one move.

Ask the three follow-ups. Has anybody told them? What happens if they do it right? What happens if they do it wrong? The third answer is usually the diagnosis.

Mark the five causes for your real request. Be honest about the incentive row - it is the one everybody skips, because naming it sounds like criticising the manager who is asking you for the training.

Write your fraction. A third, a half, most of it. This number goes in the b6 business case and it is what stops you being blamed for the other two thirds.

Draft the yes-and sentence. Say it out loud once. If it sounds defensive, you probably led with the diagnosis rather than with yes.

Decide which non-training action you will own. One. Usually the cheapest - a job aid, a line in a checklist, a conversation with the platform team.

Notice the trap. Making it mandatory and annual is the standard organisational response and it makes things slightly worse: it converts a session for the people who need it into a compliance exercise for everybody, and b2 is about exactly what that does to a room.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and this session gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What does "could they do it if their life depended on it?" establish?

It separates "cannot" from "does not", which is the single most useful split in this discipline and takes about ninety seconds. Training only ever addresses "cannot", and a large share of requests are about "does not".

2 · At Daybreak, nothing happens to a producer who ships a breaking change - the analytics team absorbs it. Which cause is that?

This is the row people skip, because naming it sounds like criticising the manager asking for the training. It is also frequently the largest share of the problem, and no course has ever fixed an incentive.

3 · Why is "training will not fix this" the wrong response even when it is true?

Being right is not the same as being useful. "Yes, and it gets you about a third of the way - here are the other two thirds, I will take one" keeps you in the conversation, and it is a better diagnosis rather than a softer one.
