Assessment and feedback

Most quiz questions in most training test whether somebody remembers a slide. That is a different ability from the one the objective named, and the gap between them is where the illusion of a successful course lives. Tonight: writing a question that tests the actual ability, building wrong answers out of the misconceptions people genuinely hold so that choosing one is diagnostic, and giving feedback that changes what happens on the next attempt. "Incorrect" is not feedback.

Where we are

Session a2 decided what would prove the objective. Tonight you build the instrument, and the instrument is also your best teaching tool - a well-built question teaches more than the slide it replaced.

★ What you walk out with tonight. Three questions that test your objective, distractors built from real misconceptions, and a feedback line for each wrong answer that says what to do differently.

LiveFormative and summative, and why one of them is optional2 min▶
  • Formative assessment steers. It happens during, it is low stakes, and its purpose is that you and the learner both find out what is not working while there is still time to do something.
  • Summative assessment certifies. It happens at the end and its purpose is a judgement.

Most workplace training has only the second, at the end, and often only as a completion tick. That is the wrong one to have if you can only have one. A course with only summative assessment cannot correct anything - by the time it tells you something, everybody has gone home.

If your session has one assessment moment, put it three-quarters of the way through, not at the end.

Part 1 · covers the question

Testing the ability, not the slide

6 min live
LiveThree tests for a question4 min▶
  • Could somebody who can do the job get it wrong? If yes, the question has a trick in it and you are measuring your own cleverness. Remove the trick.
  • Could somebody who cannot do the job get it right? If yes - by elimination, by pattern, by remembering the emphasis you put on a word - the question is not measuring the ability.
  • Does it use the objective's condition? "Given a proposed schema change" means the question hands them a proposed schema change. If your objective has a condition and your question does not, they are testing different things.

The fastest way to fail the second test is to make the correct option the longest and most carefully qualified one. Everybody who has ever sat a badly written test knows to pick that one, and they do not have to know anything about your subject.

Part 2 · covers distractors and feedback

Wrong answers built out of real misconceptions

6 min live

A wrong option that nobody would ever choose is a wasted line. A wrong option that half the room chooses tells you exactly which misconception is in the room, which makes the question a diagnostic instrument as well as a test.

LiveFeedback that changes the next attempt4 min▶

Three parts, and the third is the one that gets left out:

  • What was wrong. Specifically, not "incorrect". "You classified this as breaking."
  • Why. The misconception, named. "The old column is still present, so no consumer has broken yet."
  • What to do differently. The rule they can apply next time. "Ask what a consumer reading today would get. If the answer is unchanged, it is not breaking yet."

And the timing, which is a design decision rather than a preference: immediate for procedures, slightly delayed for judgement. When there is one right answer, tell them straight away. When it is a judgement call, let them commit and argue first - feedback that arrives before somebody has finished thinking replaces their thinking with yours.

The version that does nothing

"Incorrect. The correct answer is B." They now know they were wrong and have no idea why, so on the next case they will apply the same reasoning that just failed. This is what most quiz feedback in most systems looks like.

Build-along

Three questions, with distractors you collected

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Take a real case. One of the ten you collected in a4. Do not invent - a real case carries the ambiguity that makes the question worth asking.

Ask the ability. Use the objective's condition. Hand them the thing and ask them to do the verb.

Collect distractors. Not invent. Ask two colleagues to answer it and write down their wrong reasoning verbatim. That is your option list, and it is better than anything you would have written.

Write feedback per wrong answer. What was wrong, why, what to do differently. Three lines each. This is the part that is actually teaching.

Test it. Give it to somebody who can do the job and somebody who cannot. If the first gets it wrong or the second gets it right, the question is broken.

Write question one from a real case. Include the condition from your objective. It will be longer than a normal quiz question and that is correct - the situation is part of the test.

Go and collect wrong answers. Ask two colleagues, write their reasoning down verbatim. Real wrong reasoning is more plausible than anything you can invent, because it survived somebody's actual thinking.

Check your correct option is not the longest. If it is, everybody picks it without knowing anything. Rebalance the lengths.

Write three-part feedback for each wrong option. Name the misconception explicitly. "You may be assuming additive changes are always safe" does more work than any amount of re-explanation.

Decide the timing. Procedure, so immediate? Or judgement, so let them commit and argue first? Write it into the plan rather than deciding in the room.

Test both directions. One person who can do the job, one who cannot. Both results are informative and the second one is usually more surprising.

Now do two more. Three questions, well made, beat twelve improvised ones - and three is enough to tell you who is lost.

If you take one thing from tonight. Collect your distractors instead of inventing them. Twenty minutes asking two colleagues gets you wrong answers that are genuinely tempting, and a question whose wrong answers are tempting is the only kind that tells you anything.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and this session gets stamped in your passport.

1 · If your session can only have one assessment moment, where does it go?

Assessment at the end certifies but cannot correct - by the time it tells you something, everyone has gone home. Formative assessment steers, and steering requires there to be road left. A baseline is useful and it is not the one to have if you only get one.

2 · Where should the wrong options in a question come from?

Invented distractors are either obviously wrong, and therefore wasted, or accidentally ambiguous. Real wrong reasoning survived somebody's actual thinking, which makes it tempting - and a question whose wrong answers are tempting is the only kind that diagnoses anything.

3 · What is missing from "Incorrect. The correct answer is B."?

Three parts: what was wrong, why, and the rule to apply next time. Without the third, the person knows their answer failed and has no changed basis for the next one. Naming the misconception explicitly does more work than any amount of re-explanation.
