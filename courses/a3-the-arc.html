The arc and cognitive load

When a room goes quiet and stops asking questions, the instinct is to reach for engagement - more energy, a story, an icebreaker. Usually the room is not bored, it is full. Tonight: how little anybody can hold at once, why a worked example teaches a beginner more than letting them struggle, when to take the support away, and the sequencing decision that makes the difference between a session that builds and one that just accumulates.

LiveThe reframe this session is built on2 min▶

Working memory - the part of your attention holding things right now - is small and does not get bigger with motivation. An expert can hold a schema change as one thing. A novice is holding the column name, the type, the consumer list, what "breaking" means and what you just said about notice periods, all separately, and there is no room left to think.

That produces the single most useful diagnostic question in course design: when your room goes quiet, ask whether they are bored or full. They look identical from the front and they need opposite responses. Bored needs more stakes. Full needs less content and more time.

Almost every time somebody adds energy to a full room, it gets worse.

Part 1 · covers load

Three kinds of load, and only one is worth having

6 min live
LiveFour presentation habits that waste load for free4 min▶
  • Talking over text they are reading. They cannot do both. Either the slide has words and you are quiet, or you are talking and the slide has a picture. This one is free to fix and it is everywhere.
  • Splitting a thing from its label. A diagram with the key underneath, code with the explanation on the next slide, a table whose column meanings were said out loud two minutes ago. Put the explanation where the thing is.
  • Saying it and also writing it and also having them read it. Redundancy feels thorough and is a load cost, because they process all three before discovering they were the same.
  • Introducing vocabulary before the thing it names. Show the schema change, let them see the problem, then name it. The reverse asks them to hold an empty label for two minutes.

None of these are about being a better presenter. They are design defects that can be fixed in the file, at your desk, with nobody watching - which makes them the cheapest improvements available in this entire course.

Part 2 · covers worked examples

Show them a solved one, then take the support away

6 min live

The instinct is that people learn by struggling. Beginners mostly do not - they learn by studying a complete solution and then trying one. The struggle becomes valuable later, and knowing when to switch is the actual skill.

Self-studySequencing decisions worth making on purpose4 min▶
  • Whole task before parts. Show the complete thing first, badly and quickly, so the parts have somewhere to attach. "We will cover the theory and it will make sense later" asks people to hold unattached pieces for forty minutes.
  • Simple to complex, on real cases. Not simplified toy cases - genuinely simple real ones. A simplified case teaches a method that does not survive the real thing.
  • Segment it. Learner-paced chunks with a stopping point beat one continuous flow, because a continuous flow gives nobody a moment to consolidate and everybody a moment to fall behind permanently.
  • Put the hardest thing about 60 percent through. Not at the start, when they have no hooks, and not at the end, when they are tired and there is no time to recover from getting it wrong.
Daybreak, sequenced

One incident (whole task, 5 min) → the six change types with two worked examples (10 min) → three changes half-done together (8 min) → ten changes alone, checklist visible (17 min) → two ambiguous ones argued out (10 min, and this is the hardest part, at 60 percent through) → the job aid (5 min).

Build-along

Sequence yours, and write two worked examples

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Whole task first. What is the fastest possible version of the complete thing? Five minutes, done badly, so the parts have somewhere to attach.

Load audit. Go through your materials for the four wasted-load habits. Talking over text, split labels, triple redundancy, vocabulary before the thing. Fix them in the file.

Two worked examples. Not one. One looks like a special case; two lets them see what varies and what does not, which is where the pattern actually comes from.

Fade. Worked, half-done, prompted, alone. Write which of your activities is at which rung, and check you are not using the same rung four times.

Place the hard part. About 60 percent through. Then check what happens if it goes badly - is there time left to recover, or does the session end on a failure?

Write your whole-task opener. Five minutes, complete, deliberately rough. For Daybreak it is one incident: the rename, the silent load, the wrong dashboard at 08:40.

Do the load audit on real materials. Open the last deck you used and count the four habits. Most decks have all four, and every fix is free.

Write worked example one, out loud. Narrate every step including the ones you would normally skip because they are obvious to you. Those are the steps beginners are missing.

Write worked example two, deliberately different. Different in the surface features, same in the method. That contrast is what turns two examples into a pattern.

Label each activity with its rung. If everything is at rung 4, beginners will flounder. If everything is at rung 1, competent people will leave. Most sessions in the wild are all one rung.

Place your hardest moment and check the recovery. If somebody gets it wrong at 60 percent, they have time to try again. At 95 percent, they go home having failed, which is the last thing they remember.

Ask the diagnostic out loud once. Next time a room goes quiet: bored or full? Then respond accordingly, rather than reaching for energy by reflex.

If you take one thing from tonight. Two worked examples, not one. It is twenty minutes of preparation, it costs nothing in the room, and it is the difference between a group that has seen a method and a group that can apply it.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and this session gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Your room goes quiet halfway through. What should you check first?

Bored needs more stakes; full needs less content and more time. Adding energy to a full room makes it worse, and that is the default reflex - which is why the diagnostic question is worth having ready before you need it.

2 · Why give beginners a fully worked example instead of letting them try first?

Struggle becomes valuable once there is something to struggle with. Before that it produces guessing and the wrong lesson from being wrong. The skill is knowing when to fade the support, which is why there are four rungs rather than a rule.

3 · Why two worked examples rather than one?

A single example is indistinguishable from an anecdote. The contrast between two - different surface, same method - is what makes the method visible as a method, and it costs twenty minutes of preparation rather than any time in the room.
