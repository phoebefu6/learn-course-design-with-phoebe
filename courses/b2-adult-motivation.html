Part 1 · covers the three needs

Relevance, autonomy, competence

6 min live

These are not motivational slogans. Each one has a specific failure mode in a training room, and each has a cheap fix that costs minutes rather than budget.

LiveThe first five minutes, which decide the rest4 min▶

Same rule as the opening line in learn-communication-with-phoebe session 5, and for the same reason: people decide early whether this is theirs.

  • Start from their problem, not your topic. Not "today we are covering data contracts". Instead: "on 14 August, thirty-four dashboards were wrong before anybody noticed. Some of you got paged. We are going to make that specific thing less likely."
  • Say what you are not going to do. "This is not a lecture about why contracts matter - you already agree." Naming the thing they were dreading removes it.
  • Give a real choice in the first five minutes. "There are ten cases and we will get through seven. You pick which three we drop." Small, genuine, and it changes the posture of the room.
  • Say how long, and mean it. "Forty minutes and I will finish at 10:40." People give attention much more freely when the end is visible.
Part 2 · covers mandatory

What compliance actually buys you

6 min live

Making something mandatory is the standard organisational response to the problem you diagnosed in b1. It is worth being precise about what it delivers, because it does deliver something - just not the thing anybody wanted.

LiveThe expert in the room, who is not wrong4 min▶

Somebody in your session has been doing this for eleven years and has a method. Your session implicitly says their method needs changing. That is a competence threat, and the sharp comment at minute six is what it sounds like.

  • Honour it out loud, first. "Most of you already check this informally. What we are doing is making it explicit so it survives somebody being on holiday." Their method is not wrong, it is unshared - which is true and much easier to accept.
  • Give them a role. Ask them to review a pair's classification, or to argue the other side of an ambiguous case. An expert with a job is your best asset in the room; an expert with nothing to do is your biggest risk.
  • Use their objection as material. "That is a good challenge, let us take it as case eleven." The room learns more from that than from your prepared example, and the objector is now a contributor.

What does not work: overruling them with authority, or ignoring the comment and continuing. The room is watching how you handle it and will calibrate their own participation accordingly, in the first ten minutes.

Build-along

Write the first five minutes

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Their problem, specifically. A date, a number, a thing that happened to them. "On 14 August, thirty-four dashboards were wrong and some of you got paged."

Not this. Name the thing they were dreading and remove it. "This is not an hour on why contracts matter."

A real choice. Small and genuine. Which cases, what order, which part to skip. Fake choices are worse than none - people detect them instantly.

The clock. How long, and the finishing time, said out loud. Then finish then. Attention is cheap to buy and expensive to lose.

The expert. Honour the existing method in one sentence, and decide now what role you will give them.

Write your opening word for word. Five sentences maximum. This is the one part of a session worth scripting, for the same reason as the opening line in the communication course - you are otherwise composing while speaking, in front of people who are deciding whether to listen.

Find your specific incident. A date, a number, a consequence somebody in the room felt. Generic openings produce generic attention.

Write your not-this line. What are they expecting that you are not going to do? Say it in the first minute and watch the shoulders drop.

Design one genuine choice. Then check it is real - would you actually run it differently based on their answer? If not, do not offer it.

Write the sentence that honours the existing method. "Most of you already do this informally; we are making it explicit so it survives a holiday." Adapt to your context, keep the shape.

Decide the expert's role in advance. Reviewer, devil's advocate, case contributor. Deciding in the moment is how it becomes a contest.

If your session is mandatory, write the honest sentence. "This is required and the record matters. I have made it forty minutes rather than ninety, and the third part is genuinely useful." Respect buys more attention than pretending does.

If you take one thing from tonight. Start from something that happened to them, with a date on it. It costs one sentence and it is the difference between a room that is present and a room that is compliant.

  2. Find your specific incident. Real date, real number. Ask somebody if you do not have one.
  3. Design one genuine choice and check you would actually honour it.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and this session gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What does making training mandatory reliably produce?

Mandatory delivers exactly what it is designed to deliver: evidence that people attended. When the record is genuinely the point - regulatory, safety, legal - say so plainly and keep it short. Pretending it is a development opportunity is what people can tell, and it costs you the next session.

2 · Someone with eleven years of experience says sharply "we already do this". What is the best response?

The comment is a competence threat, and their method usually is not wrong - it is unshared, which is a much easier thing to say and to accept. An expert with a role is your best asset in the room; the same person with nothing to do is your biggest risk, and the room is watching how you handle it.

3 · Why open with a specific incident rather than with why the topic matters?

Almost every mandatory course opens by persuading the room of something the room already agrees with, which spends the five minutes that decide everything. Relevance is the doubt, not importance, and specificity is what settles it.
