Learn Course Design with Phoebe

Almost all professional training produces a room full of people who enjoyed it and a Monday in which nothing changed. This course is about that gap. Twelve sessions in two tracks: designing learning that produces a behaviour, and getting it used inside an organisation that did not ask for it. In track B a transfer board shows where a trained skill actually goes over ninety days, and the biggest lever on the whole board is not something that happens in your session at all.

The running project ☕

Daybreak keeps breaking its own dashboards when a producer renames a column. Somebody has decided the answer is a training session on data contracts. Across twelve sessions you find out whether it is - and if so, design one that survives contact with a Monday.

Track A · six sessions

Designer ✏️

Design the learning. Objectives that can be failed, assessment before content, an arc that respects working memory, activities that produce the behaviour, and materials for the room you actually get.

🎯 a1 · easy

Objectives that can be tested

"Understand our policy" is not an objective, because nobody can fail it. Observable verbs, a condition and a criterion - checked live by a linter that reads your real text.

▶ Start here 🔄 a2 · easy

Backwards design

Decide how you would know first, then choose the content that serves it. The question that deletes more slides than any other, and the not-this list applied to a curriculum.

assessment first 🧊 a3 · medium

The arc and cognitive load

Working memory is the binding constraint, and most bad training is a load problem wearing a motivation costume. Worked examples, fading, sequencing, segmenting.

fade the scaffolding 🛠️ a4 · medium

Activities that produce the behaviour

Practice beats exposure, retrieval beats re-reading, spacing beats cramming - including the part where the room feels harder and the results get better.

harder in the room ✅ a5 · hands-on

Assessment and feedback

Questions that test the objective rather than the slide, distractors built from real misconceptions, and feedback that changes the next attempt. "Incorrect" is not feedback.

diagnostic wrong answers 📋 a6 · hands-on

Materials and the room you get

Forty minutes not sixty, a broken projector, four people on a link. The one-page job aid they keep, and materials that still work without you standing next to them.

the real room
Track B · six sessions

Delivery and L&D 💼

Get it used, and prove it changed something. Needs analysis, adult motivation, running the room, transfer, measurement, and the internal sale. This track absorbed the planned corporate-training course - the source map says why.

🔍 b1 · easy

Needs analysis

The training request is usually not a training problem. Five causes of a performance gap, only two of which are training, and the one question that saves the programme.

Relevance, autonomy, competence - and why mandatory training reliably produces completion rather than change. Plus honouring the method they already have.

why mandatory fails 🎤 b3 · medium

Running the room

the run of show 📈 b4 · medium

Transfer

What is honest here 🧾

One widget is a measurement and one is a model, and each says which it is on its own footer.

ClaimStatus
The objective linterReally measured. String analysis on the objective you type: non-observable verbs found by name, condition present, criterion present, short enough to test
The transfer board numbersA teaching model. The shape follows well-replicated findings - unpracticed material decays fast, retrieval and spacing beat re-exposure, and what happens after the room matters more than what happens in it. The digits are calibrated so the trades are visible, and they are not a measurement of your programme
The verb listsChosen, not derived. 65 observable stems, inflected automatically, and 19 non-observable ones. They exist to make you look, not to make the decision
The four-level evaluation modelTaught with its critique rather than as received wisdom
Daybreak and its training requestA teaching scenario. The measurement code is real; the company is not
